## Overview

The Abada is a legendary creature from the folklore of the Kongo-speaking peoples of Central Africa, first described in European sources by 17th-century Italian Capuchin missionary Giovanni Antonio Cavazzi da Montecuccolo during his time in the kingdoms of Kongo, Matamba, and Angola. Unlike the European unicorn, the Abada is described as having two crooked horns rather than one, and is roughly the size of a small donkey, with a tail likened to that of a wild boar. The animal is generally portrayed as small, shy, and elusive, and its horns are believed to neutralize poison and to cure disease. The creature is also called Nillekma or Arase in some accounts. The name 'Abada' itself appears to stem from a wider Afro-Eurasian terminology for rhinoceros and other large-horned animals — Portuguese 'bada / abada' and Malay 'badak' both refer to the rhinoceros — which European writers conflated with local Kongo descriptions of a horned forest beast.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: The name was popularized in European literature through 17th-century Portuguese and Italian missionary accounts of the Kongo. The Portuguese 'bada' and 'abada' (from Malay 'badak', 'rhinoceros') were applied loosely to large horned animals; in Cavazzi's Kongo account it became attached to a two-horned, unicorn-like beast.
- **Meaning**: Likely derived from Malay 'badak' (rhinoceros) by way of Portuguese maritime trade vocabulary; in the Kongo context the term came to refer specifically to a two-horned mythical animal whose horns acted as an antidote.
- **First use**: 17th century (Cavazzi's writings on Kongo, Matamba, and Angola)

## Theories & Explanations

### Scientific

**Misidentified or composite ungulate**

Some commentators suggest the Abada may reflect Kongo descriptions of real two-horned animals (e.g. various antelopes, or the rhinoceros via the Portuguese term abada/badak) misunderstood by European listeners as a single mythical species.

### Skeptical

**Folklore, not zoology**

The Abada is best understood as part of a global 'magical horn' folklore complex rather than as a zoological claim, paralleling unicorn beliefs in Europe and Asia.
